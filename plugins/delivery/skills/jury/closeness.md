# Closeness to the awarded level

What a drawing of each kind shows on an awarded board of an urban-design competition, for a jury that has no reference images. A jury walks past dozens of boards and judges images before it reads text. So score what you see from across the room first: what is drawn and what is left out, the amount of detail, how the drawing fills its frame. Then come closer and check that it holds up.

| Score | Means                                                            |
| ----- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 5     | Could stand on an awarded board: everything in "a 5 shows" below |
| 4     | Close; one gap you see at once                                   |
| 3     | The right content, but less detail or a weaker hierarchy         |
| 2     | The right kind of drawing, with much less in it                  |
| 1     | Nothing alike, or missing                                        |

Score every drawing the brief requires, and each board as a whole: a jury meets the board before any drawing on it. Score the elements first (what is drawn), from the list for its kind. Then check its composition against "Every image" below, and a board also against "Each board as a whole". Lower the score by one when it fails two or more of those checks, and never raise it for composition alone.

## Every image

- **One idea per image.** You can say in one sentence what it shows, and that sentence belongs to the entry's storyline. An image that shows something the story doesn't need is decoration.
- **Hierarchy.** The eye lands first on the new design, then its public centre, then the context. The new is darkest or most coloured, the context recedes in pale tones, and only one thing per image carries the accent colour.
- **Buildings against ground.** Buildings, open space and streets read apart at a glance; the ground is never as loud as what stands on it.
- **Frame and crop.** The subject fills the frame and sits on purpose, cut where the context stops mattering. No empty corners, no subject squeezed into a strip, no crop that cuts the design.
- **Density of detail.** As much detail as the scale can carry: trees, people, surfaces and uses where they can be seen. An image emptier than its scale allows reads as unfinished, one fuller than it allows reads as noise.
- **Legible from a distance.** The main shapes and the key labels read at arm's length on a printed board. Text inside the image is short, set in the board's font, and never smaller than the board's body text.
- **Graphic tools complete.** Plans carry a scale bar, a north arrow and the scale in the caption, and a legend only for what isn't self-evident. Every image has a caption.
- **One family.** The same palette, line weights, tree symbols, people and fonts as the board's other images. A drawing in a different style breaks the board, however good it is alone.
- **Nothing technical showing.** No file names, layer names, software defaults, pixelation, cropped legends or placeholder text.
- **Honest.** The image shows what the plans show. A view that promises a place the plan doesn't draw counts against the entry.

## A 5 shows

- **Figure-ground.** Black buildings on white at the brief's scale, the district in its city, the new buildings distinct from the old, the city fading towards the edges, the frame filled. Composition: the site sits near the middle, cut wide enough to show what it joins (centre, river, rail, landscape).
- **Site plan.** A pale, quiet ground (green-grey, not flat colour fields), white or light buildings with a fine outline or shadow, every tree as a crown, paths, courtyards and the texture of meadows, fields and water, storeys as small numbers, one accent colour only for the public centre. Almost no text on the drawing. Composition: it is the board's anchor and fills about half a board, cut tight to the site and its edges, with the context drawn in but quieter.
- **Detail area (1:1,000 or 1:500).** Each use labelled, entrances, the surfaces of squares and streets, yards and gardens drawn, the edge between public and private ground, single trees, play areas, and where the brief asks for it the mobility point drawn in full (bus bays, drop-off, bike parking). Not the site plan enlarged. Composition: framed on the one place it explains, with the labels around the drawing rather than over it.
- **Perspective.** At eye level, with many people doing things, plants in the foreground, the programme readable (a market, play, terraces), soft light; a drawn or collaged look rather than a bare render. Every perspective shows a different place. Shouldn't be photorealistic, but in a conceptual style. Composition: a foreground, a middle ground with the main space and a background that closes the view; the horizon at eye height; the sky a small, quiet part of the frame.
- **Isometric.** The district or its centre filling the frame, roofs, trees and the surrounding city in detail, uses labelled; never thin extrusions on flat ground. Composition: one view angle across the board, the new buildings light against a paler city, the edges of the drawing fading out rather than cut.
- **Building types.** Four to eight types at one scale, each an ensemble with windows, roofs, balconies, yards, trees and people, each with a name and a short line on who lives there (the housing mix). Composition: one row or grid at one angle, equal spacing, the names and lines aligned under each.
- **Section.** Long and thin across the whole site, on the real ground, with the buildings behind in elevation, trees, people and uses. Composition: a bold cut line against lighter elevations, the sky left white, vertical exaggeration only if it is stated.
- **Concept diagrams.** Four to six small pictograms in a numbered sequence, one accent colour, one short line each. Composition: the same frame, angle and base in every step, so only what changes moves.
- **Phases.** The phases on a small plan in tones of one colour, with homes or residents per phase next to each. Composition: one plan or a short row of plans at the same scale, read in order.
- **Mobility, open space, climate.** Each a small plan of one system over a muted base, lines and areas in a few distinct colours, a legend of five to eight entries. Composition: the same frame and scale for each, side by side.
- **Figures (sealing, programme, mix).** Pie or bar diagrams with their percentages, not tables. Composition: the same size and colours as the plans they count, placed next to them.
- **Text.** Short blocks in columns, a lead line per block, set beside the images they explain. No paragraph runs wider than a column.
- **The boards.** Edge to edge with no empty white, one anchor image per board, small text in columns, the same palette on every board, the title and the idea on the first. Composition: the checks below.

## Each board as a whole

- **Read from three metres.** The title, the idea and one anchor image read before anything else; a passer-by can say what the entry proposes.
- **One anchor.** One image clearly largest (on a plan board, the site plan at about half the board), a few medium, the rest small. Not many images at the same size, and no large image of something secondary.
- **Grid.** Every image, caption and text block sits on one column grid, with aligned edges and even gutters, the same on every board.
- **Balance.** Images, text and white are spread on purpose: no empty patch, no crowded corner, no image floating in leftover space. White is a margin, not a gap.
- **Reading order.** From top left to bottom right the board follows the storyline: from the idea to the plan to the detail. Each image sits beside the text and figures that explain it.
- **Images over text.** Text is short and in columns; tables, legends and figures are small and placed next to what they count. A board read mainly through its text has failed as a board.
- **Captions.** Every image has one short caption in one place and style; none is repeated or left orphaned.
- **One family across the boards.** The same palette, fonts, line weights, tree symbols and kind of image on every board; no single image in another style.
- **A sequence.** Each board is one chapter, the boards put side by side read as one story, and the strongest image is on the first board.
- **The brief's form.** Format, orientation, code box, numbering, title and language as the brief asks, and nothing that breaks anonymity.
